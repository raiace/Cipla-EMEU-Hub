Section 02 · Daily Life

Daily Life in São Paulo

The small things nobody briefed you on — until now. Voltage, WhatsApp, Sunday streets, and why Brazilians greet strangers in lifts.

🛒 Groceries

  • Main chains: Carrefour, Extra, Pão de Açúcar (premium)
  • Budget: Atacadão — excellent for bulk shopping
  • Fresh produce: Feira livre (street markets) — cheaper and fresher
  • Indian groceries: Liberdade neighbourhood and Pinheiros
  • Online: iFood, Rappi, supermarket apps — set up in Week 1
  • Hours: 8am–10pm most days; some Carrefour stores 24 hours

📱 SIM & Banking

  • SIM: Buy Day 1 at Claro, Vivo, or Tim — bring passport
  • Best for short stays: Claro or Vivo — R$50–150/month unlimited
  • Banking: Nubank or Inter — open in minutes with passport + CPF
  • PIX: Instant free transfers — Brazil's version of UPI. Set up in Week 1.
  • Broadband: Usually included in serviced apartments
  • Electricity: 127V or 220V depending on building — ask before plugging in

🏥 Healthcare Basics

  • Cipla provides convênio médico — confirm scope with HR before travel
  • Always carry your convênio card — without it you pay upfront
  • Pharmacy: Drogasil and Droga Raia everywhere, open late
  • Indian medicines: Bring 3-month supply — Brazilian brand names differ
  • Full healthcare detail → see Housing section

🏠 Domestic Life

  • Faxineira (cleaner): 2× per week standard — more accessible than in the UK
  • Dry cleaning: Dryclean USA and local chains at shopping centres
  • Neighbourhoods: Itaim Bibi (business), Pinheiros (international), Jardins (upscale)
  • Safety: Stay in expat-friendly areas — Itaim Bibi, Moema, Jardins, Pinheiros
  • Sundays: Streets quieter, many shops open. Feiras and parks busy.

🍖 Churrasco Culture

  • National social institution — far more than a BBQ. This is where trust is built.
  • Red/green disc at table: green = keep the meat coming, red = stop. Always learn this before you go.
  • Accepting a churrasco invitation builds professional relationships faster than any meeting
  • Botecos (street bars): the after-work social hub. Going matters more than what you drink.
  • Caipirinha: national cocktail. Non-alcoholic options always available — just ask.

🥗 Vegetarian & Indian Options

  • Vegetarian growing fast in São Paulo — look for 'sem carne' or 'vegetariano' on menus
  • Vegan options strong in Pinheiros, Vila Madalena, Itaim Bibi

  • Indian groceries: Liberdade neighbourhood (São Paulo's Asian district) and Pinheiros
  • Brazilian staples to enjoy: açaí bowls, pão de queijo, arroz e feijão (rice and beans)
Tipping: 10% service charge is often added automatically — check the bill before adding extra. PIX is widely accepted even at street food stalls. Carry R$50–100 cash for feiras and small shops.

Section 04 · Housing

Where to Live

São Paulo's expat areas are safe, well-connected, and close to business centres. Start with HR support — private rentals require CPF and local documentation.

🏨 Serviced Apartment (recommended)

  • Fully furnished, bills included, flexible term
  • Cost: R$6,000–12,000/month (1BR)
  • Best areas: Itaim Bibi, Jardins, Moema, Pinheiros — safe and expat-friendly
  • Arranged through Cipla HR for short assignments

🏩 Hotels (<2 weeks)

  • Marriott, Hilton, and local boutique hotels in Itaim Bibi
  • Reliable, centrally located, business district
  • Airbnb available — stick to Itaim Bibi, Jardins, or Pinheiros
  • Check host ratings carefully and verify reviews before booking
⚠️ São Paulo rentals require CPF, proof of income, references, and a guarantor (fiador). Start the CPF process with HR before you arrive — it takes time. The rental market is competitive; begin search 4–6 weeks early with HR's vetted contacts.

🏠 Private Rental

  • Requires CPF, proof of income, references, and 3-month deposit
  • Guarantor (fiador) typically required — HR can assist
  • Monthly rent 2BR: R$4,000–7,000 + utilities R$400–700
  • Best areas: Jardins, Itaim Bibi, Moema, Brooklin — all expat-friendly

💼 HR Global Mobility

  • Essential for São Paulo housing — use HR's vetted contacts only
  • CPF registration and bank setup assistance
  • Visa and tax equalization support
  • Contact 4–6 weeks before arrival — never leave housing to the last week

Healthcare in Brazil

🏥 Private — Convênio Médico (use this)

  • Cipla provides convênio médico — confirm scope with HR before travel
  • Hospital Albert Einstein (Morumbi) — world-class, English-speaking, most recommended for expats
  • Hospital Sírio-Libanês — top private, multi-lingual staff, excellent emergency care
  • GP: 'clínica médica' within your network — appointments in 1–2 days
  • Always carry your insurance card — without it you pay upfront

🏛️ Public — SUS (free but limited)

  • Free for all residents including visa holders
  • Long queues — not practical for short-term expats
  • UPA (Urgent Care): Free, 24 hours — good for non-critical issues
  • SAMU: Call 192 for ambulance — free emergency service
  • Dengue: Real risk in summer. Know symptoms: fever, joint pain, rash. Seek care promptly.

Section 08 · Customs & Language

Social Customs & Portuguese Essentials

You don't need to be fluent. You need to be warm, open, and willing to try. That's it.

01 · Norm

Physical warmth — cultural, not personal

Brazilians are famously warm and tactile. Abraços (hugs) and beijo (a brief cheek kiss as greeting) among friends and colleagues are common as trust builds. These are cultural greetings — expressions of trust and inclusion. They carry no romantic or sexual meaning. Handshakes are standard on first business meetings; warmer greetings follow naturally. Accept graciously — refusing can be misread as cold or rejecting.

02 · Norm

Jeitinho Brasileiro — finding a way

Brazil's cultural tendency to find creative solutions around obstacles — a workaround, a favour, a flexible interpretation. This is not corruption; it is adaptability and resourcefulness. If something seems impossible, ask a Brazilian colleague — they may find a way you hadn't considered. Reward this flexibility with appreciation, not frustration.

03 · Norm

Time flexibility — and what it means

Social events start 15–30 minutes later than stated. Business meetings may also run late. Being on time to a social event in Brazil means you'll be the first person there. For formal work meetings: still aim to be on time — punctuality for professional meetings is respected, even if not always matched.

Section 09 · Work Culture

The Brazilian Workplace

Warm, relationship-first, hierarchical with humour, and powered by WhatsApp. If this surprises you, read on.

01 · Core Principle

Relationships before business

Business in Brazil is built on personal trust (confiança). Brazilians invest time in knowing you before discussing work. Small talk, personal questions, and social events are not optional — they are how credibility is built. Jumping straight to business is seen as cold and transactional.

02 · Core Principle

Hierarchy with warmth

Brazilian organisations are hierarchical, but leaders are warm and approachable. Respect titles and seniority — address senior people formally until they invite informality. Emotional intelligence and social connection are valued at all levels, alongside professional competence.

03 · Communication

Expressive and direct communication

Brazilians communicate with energy — gestures, expressions, tone. Interrupting can signal enthusiasm, not disrespect. Feedback can be more direct than you expect from Indian corporate culture. If someone disagrees, they tend to say so — read tone, not just words.

04 · Social Norm

Lunch and after-work are work

Lunch is a social occasion — eating at your desk alone in Week 1 is read as standoffish, not productive. After-work churrascos, botecos, and events are where relationships and trust are built. These are not optional extras — they are professional activities in Brazilian workplace culture.

For Indian Professionals — Read This Carefully

Less formal than Indian corporates — first names are used after introductions at most levels
Much more social — team lunches, churrascos, and after-work are part of the job
Meetings may start 10–15 mins late — this is normal for internal meetings; formal client meetings expect punctuality
Personal questions are normal — family, origins, impressions of Brazil are common warm-up conversation
WhatsApp is used for work — respond during working hours as you would for email
Physical warmth as greetings (abraço, beijo) is cultural — it signals trust and inclusion, not romantic intent
Jeitinho brasileiro — when something seems impossible, ask a Brazilian colleague. Creative solutions exist.
